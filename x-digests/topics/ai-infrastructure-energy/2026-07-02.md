# AI Infrastructure Energy
**Date:** 2026-07-02

## Key Examples
Data center electricity consumption stood at approximately 415 TWh in 2024, representing about 1.5% of global electricity, with projections showing a rise to roughly 945 TWh by 2030 — equivalent to Japan's entire current electricity use. In the US, data centers are expected to soon surpass the combined power draw of the aluminum, steel, cement, and chemicals industries. Power demand is forecasted to increase from ~115 GW today to 240–280 GW by 2030, with individual facilities commonly drawing 100–300 MW. @dmxplx and @MiningPropChile shared these IEA-linked projections and capacity calculations in detailed threads.

Local surface temperatures around data centers rise by an average of ~2°C, with some hotspots reaching increases as high as 9°C, while cooling systems create a water footprint equivalent to the annual domestic needs of ~1.3 billion people in one projection. Generating one AI image reportedly uses over 1,000× the energy of a simple text search. @ECOWARRIORSS and @zero_ssufuri posted the environmental impact numbers, sparking debate in replies about whether the figures are overstated for clusters near residential areas.

Around 20% of planned data center projects face delays due to long lead times for transmission lines and equipment, highlighting that grid and transmission bottlenecks are the real constraint beyond generation capacity. AI itself could unlock up to 175 GW of existing transmission capacity through optimization. @dmxplx emphasized this transmission focus in follow-up discussion.

Meta has committed nearly $183 billion to AI infrastructure, including large data centers in Louisiana and Ohio, and is reportedly preparing to sell excess compute capacity. Anthropic scaled from ~$9B to $44B ARR in five months by securing dedicated AWS Trainium capacity at below-market rates and expanding inference gross margins from 38% to over 70%. @thearslaniqbal and @MilkRoadAI detailed the corporate capex moves and economics.

## Insights
The shift in focus from model performance to power, transmission, and commodity demand (copper, lithium, rare earths) reveals that control of energy infrastructure and efficient software layers may determine winners more than raw intelligence, as buildout speed becomes the limiting factor.

Local environmental impacts like heating and water use create second-order political and community resistance that global demand projections alone do not capture, forcing companies to address site-specific constraints in addition to national grid planning.
