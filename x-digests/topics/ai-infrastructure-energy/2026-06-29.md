# AI Infrastructure Energy
**Date:** 2026-06-29

## Key Examples
Discussions reference the IEA's Energy and AI report (2025) projecting AI-focused data center electricity demand more than quadrupling globally by 2030. Data centers consumed ~415 TWh in 2024 (1.5% of global electricity, 0.5% of CO₂), with projections doubling to ~945 TWh by 2030 or ~3% of global use. In the US, data center demand is expected to surge 133% in six years, exceeding the combined electricity use of steel, cement, aluminum, and chemical industries. A single large AI training run can consume as much power as thousands of households in a year.

GPU power density is the core driver: NVIDIA H100 at ~700W TDP under load, with newer Blackwell/GB200-class GPUs reaching up to 1,200W per GPU. Racks run at 50–150 kW versus 5–15 kW for traditional CPU racks, requiring liquid cooling (direct-to-chip or immersion) as air cooling fails at these densities. Hyperscale facilities use 1–5 million liters of water per day for evaporative cooling, with Microsoft's global water use rising 34% in one recent year tied to AI scaling. Regions like Arizona, Nevada, and Texas face particular pressure.

One analysis contrasts training (historically water-intensive for cooling large runs) with inference, noting that inference is growing faster in aggregate as models become larger and use more tokens per query, potentially dominating total energy use long-term despite per-token efficiency gains. Decentralized projects leveraging idle GPUs worldwide (some crypto-incentivized) are proposed to avoid concentrating demand in hyperscale facilities.

## Insights
The combination of extreme power density and water constraints creates localized bottlenecks that global averages obscure—grid strain, higher prices, and noise during heatwaves hit specific regions hardest, even if AI remains smaller than transport or heavy industry in overall GHG share. Efficiency measures like mixture-of-experts routing and optimized models (11–33% less compute for similar results) provide meaningful offsets, but hardware TDPs continue rising faster than efficiency gains in many cases.

Co-location with renewables, nuclear restarts, and small modular reactors (SMRs) plus "behind-the-meter" deals are emerging as practical responses, while decentralized GPU networks offer an alternative architecture that spreads load but introduces new coordination and latency challenges.

## Notable Opinions
Posts express alarm over the speed of growth and "invisible" cumulative impact, with criticism of “green data centre” claims when facilities require massive backup power. Some highlight that even at projected levels the sector is manageable with innovation, but local and grid-level impacts matter more than global percentages. Pushback against rapid open-source model releases includes safety concerns around monitoring guardrails.
