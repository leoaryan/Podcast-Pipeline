# AI Infrastructure & Energy
**Date:** 2026-06-12

## Key Examples
NVIDIA and its customers are intervening directly with suppliers of specialized materials like HVLP4 copper foil and T-glass fiberglass cloth to prevent disruptions to next-gen AI server production. Demand is rapidly moving to higher-end CCL grades (M7–M9) for AI/HPC, with projected T-glass supply-demand imbalance exceeding 40% in 2026 and still around 25% in 2027. HVLP4 copper foil shortfall is estimated at 1,500 tons in 2026, widening to 2,500 tons in 2027. CCL makers are on allocation, prompting NVIDIA to use direct consignment and long-term visibility arrangements to secure capacity. The detailed supply chain analysis was posted by @jukan05 linking to a Digitimes report.

SpaceX has warned investors that water access for cooling is becoming a real bottleneck for AI infrastructure, noting that one large GPU cluster can consume as much water as a small town while thousands of such clusters are being built. Context includes OpenAI negotiating a 10-gigawatt data center in Ohio, Broadcom’s $35B compute buildout plans, and Super Micro’s GPU fundraising. Cooling innovations, including nuclear-reactor-inspired tech from an MIT startup, are highlighted as potential opportunities, with the takeaway that “the next billion-dollar AI company might not build a model. It might build a radiator.” Shared by @sol4iok.

Power is repeatedly called the primary constraint driving data center construction, with AI’s “infinite” energy demand implying that internal combustion engines aren’t going away due to continued fossil fuel use and the need for platinum/palladium catalysts in catalytic converters. Mining volumes for these metals are described as extremely limited, creating secondary bottlenecks even as GPU supply improves. Mentions of Oracle’s massive AI infrastructure backlog and SK Telecom + NVIDIA collaboration in South Korea further illustrate the scale of physical constraints beyond silicon.

## Insights
The conversation has shifted from GPU shortages to upstream physical inputs—specialized materials, water rights, cooling technology, and grid capacity—as the new rate-limiters for 2026–2027 scaling. NVIDIA’s direct supplier interventions demonstrate that frontier labs are already modeling these constraints years ahead rather than treating them as downstream problems.

Water usage for cooling represents an under-discussed second-order effect of massive GPU clusters, where the resource constraint is not electricity alone but the physical infrastructure required to dissipate heat at data center scale. This reframes AI infrastructure investment toward companies solving thermal and fluid dynamics problems rather than pure compute.
